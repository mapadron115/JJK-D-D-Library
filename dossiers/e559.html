The Immortal Game

Chess, played with shikigami at full scale. You are the king; your pieces are bound to move only as chess allows — and the binding is what makes them hit like techniques instead of furniture. Pawns screen and promote, knights jump the line, rooks own the hallway, and an enemy with no legal move left is collected by the board itself.

Core law

What the technique actually does.

Core law, the binding of the board. You summon shikigami in the shape of chess pieces through a physical chess set you carry — medium and roster in one object. The set holds sixteen pieces: eight pawns, two knights, two bishops, two rooks, one queen. There is no king piece. You are the king: if you fall to 0 hit points or are incapacitated, every piece is dismissed and the game is over. A piece may move only as its kind moves in chess, and only when you command it — that restriction is not a limitation the technique endures but a vow it is built on, and it is why carved wood strikes with the force of a cursed technique. A piece's strike is a technique attack made with your technique attack roll, against one creature whose square the piece captures to or passes through during its move; sliding pieces (bishop, rook, queen) can strike only the first creature on the line — everything behind it is shielded, and everything in front of it is blocking the shot. Between your commands the pieces are statues, but they are not harmless: a creature that leaves a square one of your pieces could capture to provokes that piece's strike, as the piece's reaction. Positioning is not decoration. Positioning is the whole technique.

Field notes. Three observations from the tournament floor. First: amateurs fall in love with the queen. Professionals fall in love with their pawn structure — eight cheap bodies that screen the king, hold the squares, and occasionally walk the length of the world and come back crowned. Second: the binding reads like a handicap until you watch a rook own a corridor. Every battlefield is secretly a board — hallways are files, doorways are chokepoints, a bridge is a diagonal with opinions — and the technique simply refuses to pretend otherwise. Third: in 1851, Adolf Anderssen sacrificed both rooks, a bishop, and then his queen, and delivered checkmate with three minor pieces. They called it the Immortal Game. The technique took the name because the game is the doctrine: material is a currency, not a score, and the player who cannot spend a piece has already lost the ones he is saving.

The pieces

Sixteen pieces. One king.

Pieces persist until destroyed or dismissed (dismiss any number, no action), and must remain within 120 feet of you or be dismissed at the end of your turn. A destroyed piece is captured: it cannot be summoned again until you finish a long rest, when the board resets. If a physical piece of the set is itself destroyed, that slot is gone until a replacement chess piece is consecrated in its place during a long rest. All pieces share: AC 13 + your proficiency bonus; saving throws equal to your technique attack modifier; immunity to poison and psychic damage; no need to breathe, eat, or sleep. All strikes deal force damage.

The Pawn

Medium · Summon 1 CE
HP 10 + 2 × level · Strike 1d8 + CE modifier

Moves up to 10 feet straight forward — the direction it faces when summoned — or 20 feet on its first move if the path is clear. It cannot strike forward: a pawn captures only into the two diagonal squares ahead of it, and a creature standing directly in front of a pawn simply stops it. The humblest piece on the board, and the only one that can become something else.

The Knight

Medium · Summon 2 CE
HP 10 + 3 × level · Strike 2d6 + CE modifier

Leaps in an L — two squares in one direction and one to the side, landing up to 15 feet away — ignoring creatures, terrain, and everything in between. It strikes the creature in its landing square. The only piece that cannot be blocked, fenced, or queued behind; it arrives the way cavalry arrives.

The Bishop

Medium · Summon 2 CE · 3rd level
HP 10 + 3 × level · Strike 2d8 + CE modifier

Slides diagonally up to 30 feet, striking the first creature on the diagonal. A bishop lives and dies on one color of the board — half the world is forever closed to it, and the other half is a firing lane.

The Rook

Large · Summon 3 CE · 3rd level
HP 15 + 3 × level · Strike 2d10 + CE modifier

Slides orthogonally — rank and file — up to 30 feet, striking the first creature on the line. A tower with opinions about corridors. The rook is why the technique's users study architecture: give it one straight line and it is the heaviest argument on the field.

The Queen

Large · Summon 6 CE · 11th level
HP 15 + 4 × level · Strike 3d8 + CE modifier

Slides in any direction — rank, file, or diagonal — up to 35 feet, striking the first creature on the line. Every pattern, every lane, one body. The strongest piece on the board and, in the hands of everyone who has ever loved her too much, the most frequently sacrificed.

Full progression

Level 1 through 20.

1Set the Board

Action · Summon one piece shikigami (see The Pieces) in an unoccupied space within 30 feet: pawn 1 CE or knight 2 CE. You may field at most a number of pieces equal to your proficiency bonus. The Move: as a bonus action or as an action, command one piece to move along its pattern and strike; you may make only one Move per turn. Opening book: when you roll initiative with no pieces on the field, you may summon one pawn for free.

Every game in history begins the same way — a pawn steps forward and the position becomes real. The free pawn is not generosity; it is the technique insisting that a game exists before anyone is allowed to fight in it. Note what you cannot yet field: no bishops, no rooks, no queen. The opening is pawns and one honest knight, and it is enough. It has been enough for a thousand years of players who were certain they needed more.

3Castling

Reaction · 2 CE · When you are targeted by an attack or an effect that forces you to make a saving throw, swap places with one of your pieces within 60 feet — both of you teleport. If an attack triggered this reaction, the piece becomes the attack's target instead. You may now also summon bishops (2 CE) and rooks (3 CE).

Chess contains exactly one move whose entire purpose is the king's safety, and it is a dance: the king steps aside, the rook takes his square, and for one motion the most important piece and the heaviest piece trade places. Everything else in the game is about the enemy. Castling is about you — which is why it exists, and why, like everything else that keeps kings alive, it is rationed.

6Promotion

Check (passive): a creature that starts its turn on a square one of your pieces could capture to is in check — it has disadvantage on attack rolls against any creature other than a piece giving it check, and you always know which creatures on the field are in check. Tempo: once per turn, you may spend 2 CE to make an additional Move. Promotion: when your pawn reduces a creature to 0 hit points with its strike, or ends its Move at least 60 feet from where it was summoned, it may promote — pay the difference in CE between the pawn and a knight, bishop, or rook, and it becomes that piece, regaining hit points equal to the difference between the two pieces' maximum hit points.

The pawn is the only piece that dreams. Every other piece is born at its final rank and dies there; the pawn alone can cross the whole board — past the skirmish, past the midfield brawl, all the way to the far shore — and be crowned for the journey. Check is the other half of the lesson: the moment a creature understands that every road out of its square is watched, the fight changes temperature. You have not touched it. You have simply made its position true.

11The Queen

You may now summon the queen (6 CE; see The Pieces). The Combination: when you summon the queen, each of your other pieces may immediately move along its pattern, without striking — the entire board rearranges around her arrival.

She is the strongest piece in the game and the one masters sacrifice with the least hesitation, which tells you everything about what strength is for. Her entrance is the combination: not a move but a chord, every piece on the board shifting at once into the position the last ten moves were secretly building. Players below master level see a queen arriving. Masters see the shape of the next five turns standing up all at once.

18Checkmate

You learn your Domain Expansion (see Pinnacle expression).

Every game ends the same way: not with a capture, but with a position — a king still standing, every square watched, no legal move anywhere on the board. The technique's final expression stops asking the battlefield to resemble a board and simply makes it one. What happens inside is not a fight. It is the last page of the scoresheet.

Pinnacle expression

Maximum, Domain, or equivalent.

The barrier falls like the lid of a presentation box. Underfoot, the ground checks itself into black and white marble, square racing square until the whole world is a board — and along the far rank your pieces rise at full height, carved and patient, while every enemy present feels the specific silence of a player who has just been shown the mate in three.

Domain Expansion, Checkmate (full-turn action, 20 CE, 1 minute, 800 clash): an enclosed Domain with a radius of 60 feet whose floor is a chessboard. Two laws govern it. Your tempo doubles: you may make two Moves per turn inside the Domain, and additional Moves bought with Tempo cost no CE. Checkmate (sure-hit): a creature that starts its turn in check within the Domain and is still in check at the end of its turn is captured by the board itself, taking 5d10 + your CE modifier force damage — no attack roll, no saving throw. The counterplay is the oldest in the game: find a legal move. Block the line, capture the piece, leave the square. The board only collects what has nowhere to go.

Feats

Build-defining options.

En Passant

Reaction · 1 CE · When a creature moves at least 20 feet in a straight line and passes through a square your pawn could capture to, the pawn strikes it as it passes (the pawn does not move). The one capture that exists only in the moment of being run past — the smallest rule in chess, and its most satisfying sentence: you should not have hurried.

The Gambit

When one of your pieces is destroyed, you may immediately make a Move with a different piece — this Move requires no action and does not count against your Moves per turn — and that Move's strike deals additional force damage equal to your proficiency bonus. Material is a currency, not a score. Anderssen surrendered both rooks, a bishop, and his queen, and checkmated with what remained. The gambit is not losing a piece. It is buying a tempo the opponent cannot afford.

Underpromotion

When your pawn promotes, you may promote it into a knight without paying the CE difference, and the new knight immediately makes a strike as part of the promotion. Once in a generation, the winning move is the weaker piece — a knight's fork where a queen would only stalemate. The technique keeps the option open out of respect for that game.

Endgame Study

While you have two or fewer pieces on the field, each of your pieces gains +2 AC, and its strikes deal additional force damage equal to your proficiency bonus. Endgames are where a position tells the truth: few pieces, no noise, every move load-bearing. The technique plays its finest chess with the least material. So did its namesake.

Rules integration

Edge cases and shared systems.

Codex rulings: Piece strikes are rolled technique attacks and can use the campaign's Black Flash procedure when otherwise eligible. The Domain's checkmate damage is a sure-hit, not an attack — it cannot critically hit or Black Flash.
Pieces are shikigami: effects that target or ward against shikigami apply to them. They are also constructs of carved wood and cursed energy — they do not breathe, eat, or sleep, and a piece uses your technique attack modifier for all of its saving throws.
Squares are 5 feet. Pattern movement ignores difficult terrain, but a slide is blocked by creatures, walls, and impassable terrain, and a piece cannot end its move in an occupied square. A piece can strike a flying creature only if that creature is within the piece's reach (5 feet) at the moment the capture would occur.
The king rule: if you are reduced to 0 hit points or incapacitated, all of your pieces are dismissed immediately. There is no checkmate against you — the game simply ends, which every chess player will tell you is worse.
The medium: the physical chess set must be on your person to summon. Losing individual pieces removes those slots until they are replaced and consecrated (see The Pieces); losing the whole set suspends the technique until you assemble another. The technique remembers the game even when you cannot field it.
